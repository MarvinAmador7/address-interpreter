# Development research, September 28

Research continues on the frozen 400,000-address corpus. Strict raw MLS field agreement remains the primary measurement. The development denominator is 315,164 eligible listing strings. The parser remains local and does not consult address reference data.

The September 26 report and its holdout result remain historical evidence. This session uses development examples only. Repeated development measurements do not establish a new holdout score.

## Experiments

The starting parser reproduced 249,694 matches, or 79.227%, with no differences from its frozen build.

| Change | Development matches | Agreement | Gain over previous accepted build |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Starting parser | 249,694 | 79.227% | — |
| Compact numeric streets and route order | 249,834 | 79.271% | 140 |
| Street hyphens and explicit route spellings | 250,203 | 79.388% | 369 |
| Secondary structure and repeated fields | 250,349 | 79.435% | 146 |
| Route fast path | 250,349 | 79.435% | 0 |
| Joined-marker punctuation and final checks | 250,349 | 79.435% | 0 |

These accepted changes lost zero matches across all five development scenarios. Together they recovered 655 raw listings, two recorded property lines and 145 generated bare-unit cases. Mean candidates per raw listing increased from 1.724 to 1.881, about 9.1%. Candidate recall alone does not measure precision, and extra interpretations add work for a future matching service.

The first experiment separates observed numeric street names and suffixes, such as `186st`, while retaining the literal reading. It permits ordinal spelling alternatives without requiring a street suffix and recognizes route numbers or letter identifiers before a route word.

The second adds spaced alternatives for hyphenated name words, state-qualified route forms, farm-to-market notation, known route abbreviations, and directional letters inside street names. An initial version failed contract tests because it also generated extra readings of numeric ranges. The accepted version narrows the hyphen rule without changing those tests.

The third experiment recovers joined markers such as `PH-A`, `APT-6C` and `BLDG-A UNIT-4`, explicit secondary phrases before a suffix, decimal identifiers, and repeated house/directional prefixes. It retains opaque identifiers and distinct unit information. A test expectation initially failed TypeScript checking; that unscored cycle remains in the journal.

The geographic development cohort reached 190,284 / 235,742, or 80.717%. The difficult-format cohort reached 60,065 / 79,422, or 75.628%. Combined development still needs 1,783 matches to reach 80%.

The largest raw-listing gains by state were Texas, 74; Michigan, 73; Indiana, 54; Kentucky, 47; Missouri, 43; and North Carolina, 40. These are gains on the fixed development sample, not estimates of national state-level accuracy.

The route fast path skips state-name and highway transformations for ordinary names. A direct comparison found identical tokens, diagnostics, candidates, assumptions and source spans on all 319,925 nonempty development listings before and after that optimization. A separate full five-scenario evaluation also found zero gains and zero losses.

The final punctuation check covers periods around joined secondary markers, including `PH-A.` and `Apt.-6C.`. Early token-shape checks avoid unnecessary repair work. These checks have their own cycles in the run journal.

Both final cycles passed all five development comparisons without losing a match. The final parser is frozen at `.local/parser-research-20260928/index.mjs`. The last held-out score remains the September 26 measurement, 79.523%; it is not a validation of this newer parser.

A local timing check used every sixteenth nonempty development listing, 19,996 inputs, with a full-sample warmup and eight alternating trials per parser. Median parse time rose from 273.5 ms for the starting build to 304.5 ms for the final build, about 11.3%. Individual trials varied, so this is a local cost observation rather than a throughput guarantee. The new grammar improves recall at the cost of more candidates and some additional runtime.

## What the state breakdown reveals

At the starting build, Connecticut had 114 matches among 2,256 eligible development listings. Of its 1,741 suffix-only failures, a lexical scan could not find the expected suffix in 1,739 inputs. Across all states, the same scan found 19,696 such cases among 21,390 suffix-only failures.

This scan normalizes the input tokens with the independent suffix reference and checks for the expected suffix. It is a diagnostic, not an adjudicated error count or a proven accuracy ceiling. Names, aliases and unusual formatting can complicate the comparison. The parser cannot know an absent registered suffix from the input alone; adding guessed values would inflate candidate recall without evidence of a correct address.

The remaining work includes distinguishing these source discrepancies from recoverable formatting, evaluating additional named and compound secondary forms, and measuring the cost of added alternatives. The combined 80% target remains unmet.

## Guidance and evidence

The package gate passes 334 tests, strict TypeScript checking, ESM/CommonJS builds and packed JavaScript/TypeScript consumers on Node.js 24.21.0. Seeded invariants now include the new compact street, joined secondary, decimal, slash and hyphen forms. No holdout examples were read or rescored during this session.

USPS distinguishes significant hyphens in address numbers from hyphens in street names, which may be represented with spaces. The implementation applies its new spacing alternative only to parsed name words and lettered routes. [Publication 28, section 232](https://pe.usps.com/text/pub28/28c2_013.htm).

Route expansion uses observed route qualifiers and identifiers. It includes forms described in [Publication 28, Appendix F](https://pe.usps.com/text/pub28/28apf.htm). It does not infer a missing county, state, or federal route designation from a bare highway number. Registered numeric street spellings still require reference data, so the parser preserves spelling alternatives rather than choosing a preferred spelling. [Section 235](https://pe.usps.com/text/pub28/28c2_016.htm).
